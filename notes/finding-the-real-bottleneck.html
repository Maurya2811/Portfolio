Finding the real bottleneck in a goods-to-person system

In a goods-to-person (GTP) warehouse, robots bring storage totes to a pick station, an operator picks items into order totes, and the storage tote goes back. When throughput falls short, everyone has a theory: the robots are slow, the network is flaky, the operators need training, the software needs more pods. On a recent project, picking went from about 800 to 1,200 totes/hr. None of those theories was right, and no hardware was added. This note covers the method, which carries over to any multi-stage pipeline.

Measure dwell time per stage, not averages per service

A tote's life is a sequence of stages: requested, retrieved, in transit, queued at station, at station, picked, returned. Each service sees only its own slice, and service-level latency metrics look fine because each service is fast at what it does. The waiting happens between services.

So the first step is to record a timestamped event at every stage boundary, keyed by a correlation id. With that you can compute dwell time per stage: how long totes actually spend in each state, as a distribution (p50 / p95), not just an average. The stage with the largest share of total time is where to look. It is usually not where people expect.

Little's law keeps you honest

For any stable system, L = λ × W: average work in progress equals throughput times average time in the system. Rearranged, λ = L / W. If you want more totes per hour at the same work in progress, you have to cut time in the system. If you just push more work in, you raise L and W grows with it. Queues get longer and throughput stays flat.

That is a useful check against "release more orders": if the bottleneck stage is already saturated, releasing more work only makes the queue in front of it longer.

Starvation and queueing look the same from far away

The key distinction in the data:

  • Queueing: totes wait in front of a station that is busy. The station is the bottleneck. Fix it by making the station faster or adding capacity.
  • Starvation: a station sits idle with nothing to pick, while other stations have long queues. The station isn't the bottleneck. The problem is the policy that decides which work goes where and when.

Both lower throughput, and a top-line "totes per hour" chart can't tell them apart. Plot per-station idle time against per-station queue length over the same window. If you see stations idle while their neighbours queue, you have an allocation and release problem.

Why more pods would not have helped

The services themselves weren't CPU-bound. Request latency was fine and consumers kept up with their queues. Scaling them horizontally would have made fast things faster while the totes kept waiting on a decision: work was released in an order that sent too much to some stations and too little to others, and different business flows competed for the same storage robots.

Allocation policy is logic, not capacity. The fixes were policy changes:

  • Balance release across stations using their live queue depth instead of a static order.
  • Segregate business flows so low-priority work (e.g. replenishment) can't crowd out customer orders on shared automation.
  • Release just enough work ahead of each station to keep it busy (a work-in-progress cap) instead of flooding the floor.

Change safely on a live floor

Warehouses run around the clock, so policy changes went in behind flags, one shift at a time, with the dwell-time dashboard as the before/after evidence and a one-switch rollback. Each change came with a measurable claim ("p95 queued-at-station drops, idle time drops") that the next shift's data either confirmed or rejected.

Takeaways

  • Instrument stage boundaries with a correlation id before forming opinions.
  • Read distributions per stage, not averages per service.
  • Separate starvation from queueing; they need opposite fixes.
  • If services aren't saturated, scaling them is noise. Look at the policy.
